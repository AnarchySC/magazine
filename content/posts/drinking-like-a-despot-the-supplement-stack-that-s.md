Darian is nineteen and he can tell you Napoleon's resting heart rate, his reported testosterone-to-cortisol ratio, and the exact cellular-renewal capsule stack a popular neural-feed show claimed the "Supreme Administrators of History" would have taken, had they lived to see the Vita-72 slurry or the Sodium Drip packets sold between segments.

The show is called *Dead Kings, Live Chat* — two settlement comedians from the Horvath Belt, recorded over mead, ranking historical rulers on a scale they invented themselves: ruthlessness, charisma, what they call "rizz-under-siege." It is funny. Darian's mother did not find it funny when he collapsed in the hydroponics bay after three days on a 900-calorie "Conqueror's Fast," chasing a jawline he'd seen attributed, probably incorrectly, to a 16th-century Earth monarch.

I came to Kepler Drift's Clinic Row because a retired medic named Yusra Okonkwo-Lindqvist mentioned, almost offhand over tea, that she was treating "an epidemic of dead men's habits." Three boys in six weeks, all with the same supplement-induced arrhythmias, all quoting the same podcast.

"They weren't sick in the way we normally see sick," she told me. "They were optimizing. Every one of them had a 160-marker blood panel printout, a stack of capsules named after cellular longevity, and absolutely no idea why their hands were shaking."

What struck me wasn't the show, or the boys, or even the supplements — mega-corps have sold self-improvement by the barrel for as long as anyone can remember. What struck me was what happened next.

Yusra started leaving her clinic door open on Tenthday evenings. She put out a pot of actual food — barley, root vegetable, whatever the greenhouse surplus was — and let the boys who'd been coming in with palpitations sit and eat instead of measuring. Word moved the way it does here, kitchen to kitchen, no notice posted anywhere. Within a month there were eleven people showing up who'd never set foot in her clinic as patients. A retired dockworker started teaching knife skills. Someone's grandmother brought a crate of actual oranges, which nobody on Kepler Drift had tasted fresh in a year, and refused to say where she got them.

"We didn't call it a program," Darian told me, stirring the pot now instead of calculating his macros from it. "It's just Tenthday. You go, you eat, you talk. Turns out none of us actually wanted to run an empire. We just wanted someone to notice we were tired."

They figured it out together. Nobody told them to.

The podcast is still running — a new episode ranking "Most Overrated Tyrant" dropped last week, sponsored by a cellular-renewal company Darian used to subscribe to. He let it lapse. Said the oranges were better.

No administration funded the kitchen. No ministry certified Yusra's open door. It is, by every measure anyone here would recognize, just a pot of soup and a standing invitation — which is, it turns out, most of what public health ever really was.
