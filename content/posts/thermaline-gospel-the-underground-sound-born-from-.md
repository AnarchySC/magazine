Two events are happening under the same headline, and only one of them is honest.

Event one: Chairman Verrin Hask touches down on the Thresher Belt — a grain colony that's basically the galaxy's breadbasket — to announce the Harvest Thrust Relief Program. Eight billion SGC, pulled from the Galactic Agricultural Credit Authority's internal ledger (the Commodity Credit Reserve, a phrase that mostly means 'nobody outside the agency can audit this'), handed first to the big commodity haulers already wired into the subsidy grid. Specialty growers — fruit, nut, root-crop operations — get told 'eligible at a later date,' which in frontier-speak means 'after the cameras leave.'

The reason the pilots need it: thermaline, the legacy combustion fuel most grain haulers still run because full fusion retrofits cost more than the ships themselves, jumped from 3.01 to 5.61 credits per liter since the Rimward Skirmish started choking supply lanes. That's not a rounding error. That's a season's margin, gone.

So far, sympathetic. Then it turns into a show. Hask stages the signing like a reunion tour — same colony, same delegate, Roth Calder of the Thresher Belt, standing beside him in a tight re-election fight. 'Delegate Calder was the one who fought for this,' Hask tells the crowd, as if relief funding were a single man's guitar solo. Then the real hook: permanent Synth-15 fuel blending, the long-term fix every farm union has asked for, gets dangled as a prize contingent on who wins in autumn. Calder wins, you get the policy. His challenger wins, 'I'm not doing it.' That's not governance. That's merch with a release date tied to a ballot.

Out past the press risers, something actually real is happening. Call it Thermaline Gospel — the pilots named it, not me. Magnetic tape only, traded hand to hand at fuel depots because the neural feeds keep burying it under the rally footage. No chord changes to speak of, mostly a drone pitched to match the whine of an old combustion injector, over which someone sings the exact liter price they paid that week like it's a psalm. Zines circulating alongside the tapes — pilots call them Slurry Sheets — run the math the press release skips: how many hauls it takes to break even when fuel ate the harvest.

Put the two images side by side. One: a chairman, a delegate, a ribbon-cutting, a conditional promise. Two: a pilot alone in a cargo bay, recording the sound of her own engine because it's cheaper than therapy and more honest than a hearing. Only one of those is going to outlast the news cycle. The other was never built to.
