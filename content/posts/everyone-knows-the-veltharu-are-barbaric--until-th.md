Everyone at the Interstellar Assembly knows the Vel'tharu of Kesh-Orrin are not ready for Compact membership. Their High Peak Communion — children chosen for symmetry and stillness, walked for weeks toward the thin-air spires, dosed along the way with a stack of compounds their priesthood calls the Three Waters, then "released" into the ancestral net at the summit — sits in every briefing packet the Earth Unified Council circulates on the species. It is cited, usually in the first paragraph, as evidence of a civilization that has not yet earned a seat.

I want to ask a quieter question than the one the briefing packets ask. Not whether the ritual is cruel. Whether it is familiar.

In the restricted stacks of the Kel'vari Press Consortium's Terran archive — the parts the Terran Intelligence Bureau stopped flagging for review sometime around the third centennial — there is a toxicology study on eight mummified children recovered from frozen peaks across the old Andean file. Hair and fingernail tissue, preserved by altitude and cold for over a thousand Terran years. All eight carried alkaloid residue consistent with sustained coca use. Four carried traces of a fermented maize alcohol. Several carried compounds consistent with ayahuasca.

The children had been walked, over months, from a political-religious center to remote mountaintop shrines. They were chosen for what the record calls purity and beauty. At the summit, they were killed — blunt trauma, not combat, not accident — then mummified and enshrined, where family members built oracle cults around the bodies for generations.

I am not going to pretend the Vel'tharu Three Waters and Inca capacocha are the same ceremony. They are not. Different gods, different chemistry, six thousand light-years and a thousand years apart. What they share is a function, and it is a function neither culture's official liturgy ever recorded: the drugs were never about transcendence. Coca counters altitude sickness on a six-thousand-meter climb a child's body was never built to survive unassisted. Chicha dulls fear enough that a child walks the last kilometer instead of being carried. Ayahuasca does something gentler and worse — it makes the ending feel like it was always going to happen this way.

That is not spiritual architecture. That is logistics. Every species with a hierarchy steep enough to require children to climb toward their own deaths eventually discovers the same pharmacology, because the problem is never metaphysical. The problem is getting a frightened ten-year-old to keep walking.

The Assembly's trade commission will keep citing the Vel'tharu ritual as a civilizational deficiency, and Kesh-Orrin will keep sitting on the helium-3 reserve that makes everyone so eager to find them uncivilized in the first place. No one on the commission has asked why Earth's own unexhumed mountains were left out of the briefing. I doubt anyone wants to find out. It would mean admitting the standard was never about the children. It was about who gets to set it.
