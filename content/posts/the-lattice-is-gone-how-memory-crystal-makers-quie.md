I wanted to finish a rig this month. A simple one — 128 units of lattice memory, the kind I used to pay 1,060 credits for out of a Tharsis Commerce Combine storefront back in 2932. I checked the listing again before writing this. It isn't sold anymore. A third-party reseller on the same storefront wants 6,800 credits for a lesser spec. The 512-unit board I'd actually planned to build now lists at 23,811 credits and ninety-nine decicreds, because apparently someone still has a sense of humor about pricing.

Here is what changed, and it isn't mysterious — it's just contracts.

For most of a century, memory lattice pricing moved in cycles. Oversupply, crash, consolidation, shortage, boom, repeat. Painful, but it meant a patient maker could always find a low point. The fabricators — Lumen Fabrics, Hanwa Dynamics, Hyundo Combine — have spent the last two years replacing that cycle with long-term allocation contracts, LTAs in the trade press. Three to five standard years, fifty to seventy percent of total output, locked to somewhere between five and sixteen buyers. Lumen's own filings say half or more of company revenue will soon run through these contracts. They're not hiding the number. They're almost proud of it.

The buyers aren't secret, either, even when the paperwork says 'undisclosed.' They're the distributed-processing operators — Tharsis Commerce Combine chief among them — who have realized that if on-site fabrication gets expensive enough, every small operation in the Core Systems will rent cognition-cycle time instead of owning hardware. Tharsis said as much, cheerfully, in its own earnings transmission: rising hardware costs mean more customers 'considering' its cloud tier. That's not a prediction. That's a business plan filed in a public ledger.

Meanwhile Halcyon Devices raised prices across its entire mobi line and called it a '100-cycle flood' in memory pricing, as though this were weather and not a filing cabinet full of signed agreements. A console maker simply discontinued its largest-capacity model rather than sell it at a loss. The Outer Rim's Rimforge Collective and Tienlong Foundry have been expanding fast enough to challenge the old fabricators on price — and the Earth Unified Council has responded not by welcoming the competition but by investigating whether Rim-made consumer-grade crystal should be allowed into Core markets at all.

What bothers me isn't the price. Prices move. What bothers me is the structural choice to delete the low point from the cycle on purpose, so that owning your own memory becomes a luxury tier and renting someone else's becomes the default for everyone.

So this is what I'm doing instead, and I'm publishing the whole method: a reclamation rig, built from retired lattice boards pulled out of decommissioned terminals, re-annealed at low temperature to restore cell integrity. It recovers roughly sixty percent of original capacity at zero procurement cost. Full schematics, tolerances, and a parts list of things you can pull from scrap are attached below. Build one. Tell me if it works for you too — that's the only review that matters.
