The palms give it away before the placard does.

Nothing else on Wildcat Ridge Preserve grows like that — not the coastal live oak, not the blackberry brambles threatening to swallow the trail, not the drifting herd of bioengineered grazers the preserve lets wander loose because, as one volunteer steward told me, "somebody has to eat the grass, and none of us wanted the job." The palms are foreign. Planted. They're the last witnesses to the Grand Vista Convalescent Commons, standing guard over a foundation of crumbled stone for longer than most of the colonies I've visited have existed at all.

It started, the placard says, in 2614, with a physician named Dr. Hendrik Vael who decided — on his own authority, which is to say on none at all — that the sick and the strange of the old Pacifica coast deserved somewhere gentler than wherever they'd been. No Colony Administration asked him to. No licensing board certified the attempt. He bought the hillside, planted orchards, dug a spring line, kept a dairy herd and a poultry run, and filled the place with what his own brochure called "an abundance of fresh, wholesome, nourishing foods, so essential to the restoration of health." People who couldn't manage elsewhere came and, by most accounts, managed here.

"My great-grandmother used to say they weren't locked away, they were just living somewhere nicer than she was," said Prea Oduya-Finch, who grew up on a neighboring plot and now leads informal walking tours of the loop on weekends, unpaid, because she likes the questions people ask. Some neighbors called it the crazy house. Others just called it beautiful — gardens, conservatories, a view that still stops hikers mid-step. The patients, by every account I could find, were quiet fixtures of a community that had decided, without anyone filing a decree about it, that they belonged there.

Dr. Vael ran the Commons for thirty-four years. He died fighting a brush fire that threatened the hillside in 2648 — the kind of death, people kept telling me, that fit the kind of life he'd had. What strikes me isn't that it worked without a charter. Plenty of things do. What strikes me is what happened after.

His siblings inherited the Commons and tried to keep it going out of loyalty rather than devotion, which turned out to be a very different fuel. There was no institution behind Dr. Vael — no succession plan, no board, no mechanism for passing on a thing that had only ever existed because one person decided to carry it. By 2663, the last sibling was dead, no heirs claimed the land, and the hillside did what hillsides do when nobody's watching: it grew back over everything. Fire finished what time had started in 2677.

I asked Prea what she thought the ruins were actually a memorial to. She thought about it longer than I expected. "Not the sickness," she said finally. "The feeding of it. Someone decided these people deserved dairy and gardens and a view, and for a while, that decision was enough to run a whole hillside on."

The placard doesn't say what replaced the Commons, because nothing did. Just grazers, brambles, and palms a long way from home, standing where someone's devotion used to be the only law on the hill.
