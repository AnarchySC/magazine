I've spent the better part of a season riding supply haulers between habitat rings, photographing things nobody asked anyone to build.

They're called grotesques when they're purely decorative, and gargoyles when they moonlight as coolant drains — stone or alloy faces grinning down from spires, bulkheads, chapel domes. The distinction is practical. The impulse behind them isn't. Somewhere between the blueprint and the finished wall, a fabricator always seems to slip something in that nobody signed off on.

At the Earth Unified Council's Assembly Dome, high above the northwest colonnade, there's a small helmeted figure the archivists call *the Old Tyrant* — submitted by a schoolchild during a design contest back when the dome went up, meant as a joke about ancient Earth strongmen. It won third place. Nobody in the selection committee seems to remember voting for it. It's stayed anyway, grinning above a carved skull, a tiny monument to the historical curiosity of involuntary rule, mounted on the very building that no longer practices it.

On Lypky Station, an architect built himself a residence covered in carved hunting trophies — his own menagerie in stone — before his credit ran dry and the Colony Administration repossessed it for diplomatic ceremonies. The animals are still there. So is the taxidermy inside, which nobody has had the heart, or the authority, to remove.

A mixed-use hab block on Amersfoort Drift wears twenty-two carved neural-feed emoji faces around its service ring — the wink, the kissy face, the heart-eyes — because the designer liked an older building across the ring that did the same thing with old gods' heads, and thought the gods deserved an upgrade.

My favorite sits on the old chapel wall at Carlisle Relay, half-hidden among the medieval-style grotesques: a small stone likeness of a dispute mediator, the kind of person my homeworld built its whole three hundred years around, who died relaying an arbitration message during a hull breach. No council ordered the memorial. The fabrication crew just added it, the way you'd mention someone at dinner so they're not forgotten.

There's a cargo-mule tribute on the Florence Spindle facing the old tradesman's row — two competing stories about why, one involving labor, one involving someone's wife. There's a deliberately backward-eared creature on a temple wall in Newcastle Ring that nobody can explain and everybody insists on explaining anyway. There's a xenomorph grinning from a renovation on Paisley Station that the stonemason never confirmed or denied intending.

And on a quiet residential block in Oak Park Settlement, above the service doors, an aging architect carved himself and his bricklayer as two cartoonish, chubby-faced little men — finally, in his last commission, laughing at his own pretensions instead of defending them.

No permit covers any of this. No ministry approved it. It just kept happening, settlement after settlement, because apparently that's what people do with a wall and an afternoon alone with it.
