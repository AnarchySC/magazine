Okay okay okay, let me draw this out for you—

Every few decades some institution rediscovers central planning and gives it a shinier box. This cycle the box is called a Prognosis Core, and the Galactic Central Bank is telling the Interstellar Assembly it can finally solve something economists have been screaming about for a thousand years: the economic calculation problem.

Picture the pitch. A Prognosis Core ingests trillions of transaction records across every settled station and spits out a prediction: this freight lane will be profitable, that asteroid claim will flop, fund this, starve that. The GCB wants to plug these predictions straight into credit allocation. No more messy entrepreneurs guessing with their own hides on the line. Just optimal distribution, computed.

Sounds efficient. It isn't.

Prediction is not judgment. A Core can tell you what happened a billion times before under similar conditions. It cannot tell you what happens the first time — because every genuine entrepreneurial act is, by definition, a bet nobody has made before with these exact variables, this exact moment, this exact risk. The Core imitates patterns. It does not own the outcome. It has no capital at stake, no bankruptcy waiting if the lane goes bust, no profit landing in its account if the claim pays off. Ownership and risk are the whole mechanism that makes prices mean anything. Strip that out and you're not calculating — you're guessing with extra steps, dressed up in confidence intervals.

And somebody always profits from extra steps dressed up in confidence intervals.

Who controls the training data? The mega-corps already sitting on the biggest transaction ledgers — Stellar Financial, Orion Trust, the usual cartel. Who gets first access to the Core's credit recommendations before they're public? Same crowd. Newly minted credits don't appear equally in everyone's account at once — they enter through the institutions closest to the Core's output, who spend them at today's prices before the dilution ripples out to the colony stations and frontier settlements that find out last, when prices have already moved.

That's the Cantillon Effect, with a prediction-machine alibi. 'The Core said so' is just a more expensive way of saying 'trust us.'

There's a second racket buried in here too: regulatory moats. Once the Assembly writes rules requiring Core-certified risk models for lending, who can afford to build a certified Core? Not the small trading houses on Kepler Freight. Only the incumbents with the processing cores and the lobbyists. The 'safety requirement' becomes a wall keeping competitors out — a moat inflated by mandate, not merit.

None of this makes the Prognosis Core useless. It's a genuinely good prediction tool. But a tool that predicts isn't a tool that decides. Entrepreneurial judgment means betting your own property on an uncertain future and living with the loss. A machine with no property and no loss isn't solving the calculation problem.

It's just hiding who's still making the bet — and who's still collecting the house's cut either way.
