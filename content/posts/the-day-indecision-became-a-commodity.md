Buried between a bio-scan ad and a hydration-salt read, there's an actual cultural movement happening.

It's called the Clarity Guild. It started as a single neural-feed transmission — The Clarity Transmission, hosted by a broadcaster named Kess Virel out of a converted freight bay on Luna Drift — and it has metastasized into retreats, merch, a supplement stack, and a guru named Jho Haddrin who will teach you, for a fee, how to decide things.

That's the whole pitch. Decide faster. With conviction. Haddrin calls it a 'trainable skill,' same as piloting or knife-fighting, and he's not wrong that indecision eats people alive — I've watched it happen to half the promoters I ever worked with. But the format selling that clarity is, itself, a monument to indecision.

Before Haddrin says one word about intuition, Virel reads seven sponsor copy blocks. A bio-scan array (160-plus tests, 365 credits, 25 off if you move fast). A nutrient stack at 35% off. Free hydration salts if you act now. A storefront platform, free trial. A neural advisor construct to 'explore ideas.' His own branded stimulant drink. A reading list nobody asked for. Seven decisions, each one already made for you by a contract signed months ago.

The show that's supposed to sell you decisiveness can't decide what to sell you — so it sells you everything, sequentially, and calls the sequence 'content.'

Forty minutes in, Haddrin pivots to something he calls 'audience capture' — the trap where a creator starts making choices based on what the crowd wants to hear instead of what's true. Real insight, delivered while a studio tech holds up applause cards and a teleprompter calibrated by neural-feed engagement metrics feeds him his next line. The man warning you about fear-shaped intuition is performing it live.

The retreats are the strangest layer — 'Certainty Immersions,' three days on a drift-station, no mobi access, guided decision drills, biometric cuffs tracking your heart rate every time you choose lunch. Attendees come back wearing little chrome pins shaped like forks in a road. It's a fashion statement now — I've spotted three of them at Kepler club nights this month alone.

The diagnosis isn't wrong. Plenty of smart people agonize themselves into paralysis, and a framework for cutting through that has real value. But watch what happens when that framework becomes seven layers of sponsored noise with a collectible pin. That's not a cure for indecision. That's indecision with a merch table.
