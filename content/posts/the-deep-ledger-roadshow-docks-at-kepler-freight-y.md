The marquee read THE DEEP LEDGER: Who Really Owns Your Debt, projected in flickering green across a repurposed ore-hauler bay on Kepler Station. Six hundred seats, 340 SGC each, sold out in eleven hours. The headline act was a former long-haul pilot turned lecturer named Varrick Osse, who spent ninety minutes explaining that colonists are, in his words, "financial livestock grazing on someone else's ledger," and that liberty, unlike a cargo manifest, "isn't something handed to you at the dock."

Nobody in the room disagreed. That is the first thing worth noting. The format runs on consensus the way a reactor runs on fuel rods — feed it agreement, and it generates heat indefinitely.

I went for the economics, not the sermon. Here is the manifest, as best I can reconstruct it: venue lease, 2,100 SGC, paid in advance by a shell distributor called Candor Transit Holdings. Candor Transit, three ownership layers down, traces to a subsidiary of Orion Trust — the same mega-corp whose colony-bond desk has spent four years restructuring Kepler's terraforming debt at 11% compounding. The firm the lecture spent ninety minutes warning the audience about underwrote the hall in which the warning was delivered.

Osse's travel came through on a different line item: 4.2 tons of recorded cassette sets, pre-sold at 85 SGC per unit, shipped ahead of him to six more stations on the circuit. Call it freight speculation — he arrives, performs, and the tonnage he sold last stop pays for the fuel to reach the next one. It is a supply chain, same as grain or water ice, except the cargo is suspicion and the margin is spectacular. A 400% markup over production cost is not unusual in distrust; distrust, unlike water ice, never spoils in transit.

What the lecture never mentioned, because it would have shortened the show, is that Kepler's actual debt structure is public record, filed with the Colony Administration and available for three SGC at any terminal. The "hidden ledger" is not hidden. It is simply boring, written in amortization schedules rather than warnings about livestock, and nobody pays 340 SGC for a seat to hear it read aloud.

Osse closed with his signature line, printed on the cassette sleeves: freedom isn't inherited, it's earned. True enough, as far as it goes. What he didn't say is that somebody earned quite a lot selling you the idea that you hadn't earned it yet.

So the manifest balances, more or less. Orion Trust funds the warning against Orion Trust. The audience pays for the privilege of being told who's profiting off them, and the man doing the telling ships his next shipment on the proceeds. Freight doesn't move on faith. It moves on paid invoices.

Who, exactly, signed the first one.
