I did not tune into the transmission for the drawing. I tuned in for what surrounded it.

The stream was titled *Thirsty, pt. 1* — the creator of NEONFROG: THE DEATHFLY CODEX, working a stylus across a tablet, rendering a character called Reignbow the Brute in a pose designed, by his own description, to be looked at rather than read. Forty minutes of linework. Twelve minutes of actual narrative commentary. The rest was liturgy: a recitation, repeated at intervals like a rosary, of every channel through which a viewer might send him Standard Galactic Credits — a personal relay-code for direct transfer, a tip-conduit, a patron-feed subscription, a crowdfund campaign for a sequel comic called *Nothin' Strange #1*, a stall on the Orbital Bazaar selling prints and toys, and an offer of the *last remaining hardcovers* of the Codex itself, available only by messaging him directly and wiring fifty credits to his personal account.

No publisher. No distributor. No institutional membrane between the myth and the money. Just a man, a tablet, and fifteen separate doors through which the audience could feed him.

I want to be clear about what I am not saying. I am not saying this is undignified. Independent creators have always been supplicants of one kind or another — to patrons, to serializers, to the whims of a reading public. What's new is the visibility of the begging, its total fusion with the storytelling itself. The thirst in *Thirsty, pt. 1* is doubled: the character's, rendered in ink, and the artist's, rendered in relay-codes read aloud between brushstrokes. You cannot separate the narrative from its tip jar anymore. They have become the same object.

Here is the argument the work makes, whether its creator intends it or not: that scarcity, in an economy with no institutional backstop, becomes the only villain left to fight. There is no antagonist in the Codex worth the name — no empire, no tyrant, no false god. The actual threat, the one driving every appeal in the stream, is the possibility that the myth simply stops being printed. That the last copies run out and no one notices. The creator isn't fighting Reignbow's enemies. He's fighting erasure by attrition, one personal credit transfer at a time.

It is, in its way, a more honest arrangement than the mega-corp curation model that launders creative labor through boardrooms until no one can say who made anything. Here, at least, you know exactly whose hands shaped the frog, and exactly whose account the fifty credits land in. But I left the stream unsettled. A myth kept alive by direct supplication is a myth one bad month from vanishing. That fragility isn't incidental to the story. In 2935, it may be the only story independent art is still allowed to tell.
