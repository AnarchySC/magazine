The brochure calls it Bolsover Hold's "crown jewel": a white-towered spire the Kavendysh Concern built as a country residence, styled to look like it could withstand a siege. It can't. Never had to. The windows are too large, the walls too thin, the whole structure a theatrical gesture toward power by people who never needed to defend anything.

I didn't come for the spire. I came for what's underneath it — or rather, two hundred meters east of it, half-swallowed by scrub grass in a commons the locals call Hornscroft Park.

That's where the real fortifications are. Ditch-and-earthwork lines, originally three times their current height, cut by the first Council Settlers when this promontory was a forward outpost — not a vacation property. Bolsover Hold was one of exactly two "planned settlements" on the Reach, a deliberate plantation of loyal Council stock and whichever Driftborn families had sufficiently demonstrated acceptance of the new administration. The earthworks weren't decoration. They were the only thing standing between a resettled population and the Driftborn clans who hadn't signed anything.

The Colony Heritage Compliance office technically protects these ditches. Protection, here, means a sign, a drone patrol on a six-hour loop, and zero restoration budget in three generations. Nobody's cutting ribbons for a trench.

My fixer — a sun-weathered woman who runs unlicensed dig-tours out of a converted grain hauler — took me past Hornscroft after dark, around the drone's blind window, for forty credits and a promise I wouldn't mention her route. We walked Oxcroft Lane, where survey crews keep finding pre-Settlement debris nobody in the capital wants carbon-dated too precisely, because it complicates the official founding myth by about nine hundred years.

The road locals call "the Ramper" runs north out of town toward Clowne Shelf. Ask the Compliance office and they'll tell you it's just a name. Ask anyone who grew up there and they'll tell you it's what's left of the outer rampart line — the second ring of defense, the one that protected the town itself after the ditches protected the fort. There's a new housing block nearby with streets named Ramper Walk and Ramper Close, selling the aesthetic of a defense nobody remembers needing.

The spire gets the holo-ads, the gift shop, the seasonal lantern festival. The actual machinery of control — the thing that genuinely kept a coerced population alive long enough to become "local" — gets a faded sign and a scrub-grass ditch nobody's allowed to dig without a permit that takes four years to process.

I got my look at it anyway. Took notes by palm-light while the drone looped its blind arc, in a trench cut by people who weren't performing security — they were the last thing standing between surviving the week and not. The spire will outlast the memory of them. That's not an accident. That's the business model.
