Let's start with the nozzle.

Two months ago I pulled a worn injector off a hauler's secondary thruster, cross-referenced the open Halcyon Driveworks fabrication spec, and printed a replacement in my public lab on a Tuesday afternoon. Worked fine. Dozens of frontier techs do this every week — Halcyon's reusable booster line built its reputation on exactly that kind of openness, a company that grew up alongside the people who kept its ships flying.

Last week Halcyon listed on the Ceres Exchange. Somewhere between the opening bell and the first dividend projection, that same injector spec got reclassified as 'proprietary thrust-control intellectual property, licensed for authorized fabrication partners only.' I tried printing it again Thursday. The compatibility check — new, silent, inserted without so much as a changelog — refused the job.

I don't understand the objection. Why wouldn't I share it? Halcyon's new investor relations office didn't have an answer either, polite as they were about not having one.

**What actually changed:**
- Fabrication licenses now require a per-unit royalty routed through Stellar Financial, Halcyon's lead underwriter.
- The old community repair wiki got a cease-and-desist disguised as a 'brand protection notice.'
- Replacement parts are now serialized and phone home to a Halcyon telemetry core before they'll ignite.
- The IPO prospectus itself — a 400-page filing I read in full so you don't have to — opened with a discount code for Aural Thread neural buds. Fifteen percent off, first-time implant customers only. A serious capital markets document, sponsored.

That last detail bothered me more than it probably should. Not because advertising is new — it isn't — but because it tells you what kind of document this really is. A prospectus is supposed to be a disclosure, a window. Bolting a sponsor read onto it turns transparency into another format for selling you something. The ships didn't get safer. The schematics didn't get better. The information just got a shareholder attached to it, and shareholders, it turns out, do not like injector specs floating around for free.

Halcyon's defense, relayed through the same press liaison, is that 'protecting proprietary propulsion architecture ensures continued innovation investment.' I've heard some version of that sentence my whole career. It always means: we found a thing people needed without paying us, and we have fixed that.

The independent fabricators I've talked to this week aren't panicking yet — the old parts still fly, the old specs still exist somewhere, copied onto drives before the lockout hit. Knowledge hoarded doesn't disappear, it just goes underground, and frontier stations have always been good at building those.

I've put my injector testing notes, failed compatibility logs included, in the usual open repository. If Halcyon's shareholders would like to explain why a part I own, on a ship I own, needs their permission to exist, my public lab channel is open, as always, to anyone willing to actually answer the question.
