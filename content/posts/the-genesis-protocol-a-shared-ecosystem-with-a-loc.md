Let's start with the good part, because there is one: pooling a directorate's worth of observatory data, accelerator logs, and reactor telemetry into something AI models can actually learn from is a genuinely powerful idea. I'd sign up for that lab tomorrow.

The Genesis Protocol — 5 billion SGC, launched by Earth Unified Council decree, now sprawled across fifteen-plus Core Systems agencies — says that's exactly what it's doing. Undersecretary for Cognition Sciences Hale Voskuyl calls artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and distributed processing cores "the three legs of the stool that transforms how science gets done." Lovely image. I asked for the stool's schematics.

What I got back was a 123-page strategy document from the Council's Office of Technology Futures describing a coming "golden age" of aligned research — meaning: funded research now has to point at Genesis priorities to get funded at all.

**Where the credits actually went**

Nearly 300 projects running through the Directorate of Stellar Energy this cycle, including a genuinely exciting effort out of the Ceres Survey Institute to unify datasets from the Varnai Deep-Field Array, the Spectral Mapping Instrument, and the Keshet Transient Relay into one trainable corpus. The astronomer running it, a colleague I trust, is thrilled about the tooling and worried about everything else — her cosmology grant, which didn't happen to rhyme with Genesis language this year, got quietly trimmed. When she asked the Directorate to explain the trade-off publicly, the answer was that Genesis only draws about 400 million SGC from the Office of Science's 8.4-billion budget. Small slice, they said. The slice, as it happens, landed on the one project that wasn't aligned.

**The quantum side is honest, at least**

Nobody's overselling quantum yet — the Quantum Development Compact's own director put it well: we're at kilometer one of a marathon toward useful, error-corrected machines, now targeted for 2939. AI is supposed to help design the hardware; quantum is supposed to someday chew through AI's harder optimization problems. That symbiosis is real and worth funding. I built a six-qubit error-correction toy on my bench last month from salvaged cryo-coils and a thrifted dilution stage, schematics below, free, because why would I not share it.

**The part that bothers me**

"Shared ecosystem" is doing a lot of work in that strategy document. Shared with whom? Access to the unified data corpus runs through a mission-alignment review. Compute allocation on the distributed cores runs through the same review. A physicist at the Interstellar Physical Society put it plainly: priorities have to be chosen, sure, but you never know in advance which obscure backwater — AI was a curiosity for decades before it wasn't — turns out to matter. Lock the backwaters out of the shared pool and you've just guaranteed you'll miss the next one.

I don't understand the question. Why wouldn't I share it? Real sharing doesn't come with a form.
