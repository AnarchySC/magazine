The press release was almost beautiful in its confidence: "The Compact had no creative, financial, or logistical involvement in the production." One sentence. Four lies, if you count generously.

I'm talking about *Homecoming of Fleet-Admiral Oduun*, the three-hour drift-epic currently vacuuming credits out of every pocket between here and the Kardashia Corridor. You know the plot even if you've never seen it, because it's the only plot the military-entertainment complex actually knows: a commander vanishes into a long engagement, is presumed lost, and returns to find junior officers feasting in his command station and courting his seat. He kills them. Rolls credits. Recruitment booth in the lobby.

I went looking for the money, because that's the job. Buried four line-items deep in the Solar Defense Compact's annual appropriations disclosure — the one nobody reads past the summary graphic — sits a program called the Narrative Cohesion Initiative, 2.1 billion SGC, description: "cultural resonance assets supporting recruitment continuity." The production company that received the largest single disbursement is also, delightfully, the same private military corp that supplied the surplus railguns used in the climactic suitor-slaying sequence — not simulated, real hardware, fired on a closed range, because nothing says artistic authenticity like a weapons manufacturer getting a tax-advantaged demo reel.

What actually interests me, more than the money, is what the writers did to the dialogue. The script team — psy-ops linguists, officially "narrative consultants" — rebuilt the Admiral's lines into modern recruitment-age vernacular. Not because the original register was boring. Because a commander who talks like *now* is a commander you can follow *now*. This is an old trick, and it's also the entire function of the myth: take something the galaxy quietly agrees is dead — the noble, sacrificial fleet commander, a figure nobody under forty actually believes in — and dress the corpse in today's slang until it walks again.

They even dug up the old hospitality code for texture. The Admiral's household suffers because the suitors broke *xenia*, the guest-host obligation, sacred and inviolable. Lovely detail. Meanwhile the Compact's actual occupied-station doctrine, applied across forty-one colony administrations currently hosting "peacekeeping" garrisons nobody asked to stay, is officially named the Xenia Protocol. The fleet is the guest. The guest never leaves. The guest writes the house rules. Somewhere a committee thought this was clever, and nobody in the room laughed, which tells you everything about the room.

Enlistment inquiries are up 19% in stations running premiere-week discount codes. The suitors, notably, never get names in the marketing. Just a body count, and a credit line for the gun.
