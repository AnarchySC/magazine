Six hundred eighty-one standard years into interstellar civilization, and the galaxy's elderly still have to prove they deserve to keep breathing — to a cognition core that gets paid more when it says no.

The Colonial Liberties Foundation spent eight months in litigation to pry loose 800 pages the Interstellar Elder Continuance Authority (IECA) did not want anyone reading. The documents concern the CULLER model — Cost Utilization Limitation & Life-extension Entitlement Reduction — the Authority's six-month-old scheme to let private cognition-core vendors decide whether a colonist over the age of 90 standard years gets an authorized procedure or a form letter.

IECA built CULLER in six months. Medical providers across four colony clusters asked it to slow down. It didn't. Its own staff now call the timeline "extremely condensed" in writing, which is bureaucratic for we knew.

The records show what that knowledge looked like in practice. Virtex Logistics, one of six selected vendors, received a formal request for information citing "determination turnaround times that exceed established guidelines," "denials that appear inaccurate or inconsistent with applicable criteria," and "inadequate customer service." Zenova Cognition Group — built on licensed Halcyon Array processing cores, the same infrastructure the Terran Intelligence Bureau uses to flag dissidents — wasn't issuing determination letters on time and, per the Authority's own letter, "may lack adequate processes for responding to and escalating provider concerns." Meridian Colonial Health Systems received two corrective-action demands and a formal non-compliance notice for failing to build a working portal — after warning IECA, in writing, before it even signed the participation agreement, that it might not be ready.

IECA knew. It signed anyway. It said nothing to the public while quietly chasing vendors for fixes. That's not oversight. That's cover.

The arithmetic is damning: by its own admission, the payment structure gives vendors "an incentive to deny as many claims as possible." One vendor's glossy application promised "human-in-the-loop workflows," "continuous model monitoring," "transparent audit trails." That same vendor denied more claims than it approved in its first ninety days. Elsewhere in the galaxy, reviewers using comparable systems have been documented spending an average of 1.2 seconds per case before rubber-stamping an algorithm's rejection. One point two seconds to decide whether a 94-year-old gets her reconstructed cardiac lattice.

When I put that figure to an IECA liaison last week, I got: "Clinical review remains central to every determination under the CULLER framework." That's not an answer. Try again.

The safeguard language that might actually explain how "human review" functions — the specific protocol one vendor submitted describing how it "ensures responsible and clinically appropriate application of automation" — was redacted before release. Not classified. Not sensitive. Redacted from an elderly colonist's own file on the mechanism deciding her treatment.

No one is arguing cognition cores can't triage paperwork. The argument is narrower: a system paid to say no, reviewed by someone who isn't looking, isn't a safeguard. It's a liability shield wearing a lab coat. The Interstellar Assembly has the authority to set hard limits on automated denial of entitled care. It has had six months. It has done nothing.
