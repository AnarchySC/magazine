The circle meets on Tuesdays, in Noriko-san's kitchen, because her table is the only one wide enough for six elbows and a teapot. I was invited after someone mentioned I 'write things down for a living,' which on Amber Hollow Station apparently counts as a character reference.

The topic that Tuesday was Emberox — the vaccine against the nerve-fire pox that half the colony's elders had just been urged, via every neural feed and ENN bulletin, to get before it was too late. The Galactic Medicines Bureau's message was everywhere: ninety percent effective. A mega-pharma called Halcyon Biologics had bought feed space on every console from here to the orbital ring repeating it.

What started the recount wasn't suspicion of the vaccine. It was a recorded message from Mei, Noriko-san's granddaughter, three systems away, who works in trial-data verification and sends voice-notes home instead of letters because 'it's faster and Grandma likes hearing my voice.' In this one, Mei read out a phrase she'd flagged at work: number needed to vaccinate. She explained it the way you'd explain anything to someone you love and don't want to lose — gently, with an example involving a jar of candy instead of a clinical trial.

So the circle got out the button tin. Hana-san, who used to run inventory for a freight cooperative before she retired to Amber Hollow, dumped out thirty-two buttons on the tablecloth. One blue button, she said, is the person the vaccine actually helps. The other thirty-one are people who took the injection, felt the ache in their arm, and got exactly the same outcome they'd have gotten doing nothing.

'Ninety percent sounds like ninety buttons,' said Kenji-san, turning the blue one over in his fingers. 'It is ninety percent of something very small, though. Of the three-point-four people in a hundred who'd get sick anyway.'

Nobody in that kitchen works for the Bureau, or for ENN's health desk, or for Halcyon. Nobody had a credential to wave. They had a tin of buttons, a granddaughter's voice-note, and four decades each of deciding, without anyone deputized to decide for them, what risks were worth taking. By the time the tea went cold they'd also pulled out the antiviral stockpile list the station keeps — a mutual-aid shelf nobody official stocked, just neighbors restocking it when they pass through the trading post — and cross-checked who already had enough on hand if the ache-and-rash version showed up instead of the nerve-pain kind.

I asked Noriko-san if she planned to get the shot anyway. She said yes, probably — the one-in-thirty-two odds were still better than none, and her knees weren't getting younger. She just didn't like being told it was ninety of something it wasn't.

Nobody called that confusion official. Nobody filed it anywhere. They just sent Mei a voice-note back, thanking her, and put the kettle on again for next Tuesday.
