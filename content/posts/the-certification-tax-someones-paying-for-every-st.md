Laika Province doesn't build ships slowly because the metal is hard to bend. It builds them slowly because the forms are hard to clear.

That's the pitch from Nadia Voskresenskaya, chief executive of Halcyon Fabrication Works and a former zero-G flight instructor for the now-defunct Meridian Launch Academy. She spent six years teaching fleet cadets how to not die in reentry simulators. Now she spends her days explaining to Colony Administration auditors why a thruster test rig that passed inspection eleven days ago needs re-inspecting because the inspector rotated stations.

Her argument, laid out on the Outer Transit Broadcast Hour this week: testing and certification aren't bottlenecks because the physics is slow. They're bottlenecks because nobody who writes the requirements pays rent on the hangar.

Consider the tonnage. A mid-size orbital tug costs roughly 40,000 SGC per week to keep docked and idle — crew wages, berth fees, insurance on cargo that isn't moving. Voskresenskaya's audit found that across nine shipyards in the Kepler-Province corridor, certification queues average 34 days longer than the actual engineering review requires. Someone is paying for those 34 days. It is never the Colony Administration issuing the delay.

The manifest doesn't match the cargo. What gets filed as "safety review" is frequently a jurisdictional handshake — three separate Administrations each requiring their own stamp on the same thruster bolt pattern, none of them talking to the other two. Voskresenskaya calls this "compliance as territory marking." Call it toll collection with better signage.

Her proposed fix isn't deregulation, a word that makes Earth Unified Council staffers reach for their mobi devices. It's parallelization — running structural, propulsion, and life-support certification simultaneously instead of sequentially, the way cargo manifests get cross-checked at a single port rather than three. She's run the numbers on Halcyon's own test floor: concurrent review cut average certification time from 71 days to 19. The ships didn't get safer or less safe. The clipboard just stopped traveling in a straight line.

What she didn't say, because executives rarely say it on broadcast, is who benefits from the 71-day version. Established yards with standing relationships to Administration review boards absorb delay costs easily — they've got the working capital and the retainer lawyers. A new fabrication outfit out of the Rimward settlements, bootstrapped on investor credit with a six-month runway, does not survive a 71-day wait on a single hull certification. The backlog isn't neutral. It's a moat, and somebody built it on purpose, even if nobody signed the blueprint.

Voskresenskaya is polite about this. She talks about "process maturity" and "stakeholder alignment," the vocabulary of someone who still needs those review boards to answer her calls next quarter. But underneath the diplomacy is a freight hauler's arithmetic: every day a vessel sits uncertified is a day someone, somewhere, is quietly profiting from the wait.

So the question isn't whether the galaxy's shipyards can move faster. Halcyon just proved they can. The question is who's been collecting rent on the slowness, and whether they'll give it up without a 71-day fight of their own.
