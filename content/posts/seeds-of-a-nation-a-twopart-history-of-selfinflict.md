Every colonial founding myth needs a good fire. Concord Station's is particularly well-documented, which is unfortunate for everyone who'd rather it stayed a legend.

In the old Mandate years, the Meridian Grand Hotel housed both the civil Secretariat and the Solar Defense Compact's regional fleet command — the sort of dual-use target that makes a private military corp's planning session write itself. Days earlier, Compact security had run Operation Clearwater, a sweep that netted 2,700 arrests and, worse for the local settlement militia, a stack of documents proving their political wing had personally signed off on sabotage operations. Paperwork, it turns out, was the real weapon.

So the New Dawn Settlement Corps — a 'defense auxiliary,' per its own letterhead — drove a delivery truck into the service bay dressed as station laborers, walked seven coolant drums packed with cryo-thermite past the kitchen staff, and left. Ninety-one people died, mostly clerks, messengers, and the unlucky man who tried to stop them at the door. Rescue crews spent three days pulling bodies out of rubble that used to be a south wing.

Nobody involved denies it anymore. They advertise it. Centuries later, Concord's sitting fleet commandant stood at the unveiling of a commemorative plaque at the Vask Heritage Hall and called the bombing's architect 'an emblem for future generations,' because, quote, 'the battle is never over.' No argument there, Commandant. The battle for next year's procurement line never is.

**Then They Tried It on the Neighbors**

A few generations on, Concord's intelligence directorate looked across the gap at the Rimward Assembly — a loose confederation flirting with a pan-colonial unification movement under a charismatic young Convener — and decided the problem needed a softer touch than invasion. Cheaper too; always check the line item.

The plan, filed internally as Operation Whisper, activated a sleeper cell of Concord-sympathizing colonists already living inside Rimward territory. Target list: a relay postal hub, two neural-archive libraries, and — ambitiously — five entertainment domes and a transit terminal, all in one coordinated night, all meant to look like the work of Rimward's own unificationist radicals. The first two jobs went off clean. The entertainment-dome job did not. A cell member's charge detonated early in his own coat pocket, which is one way to end a career.

Ten operatives were rounded up. Two took their own lives under interrogation, two were executed, the rest served time and eventually shipped home to a hero's quiet, unpublicized welcome. Concord's own defense ministry spent the next decades insisting a mid-level administrator had gone rogue and ordered it all without authorization — a story that held together about as well as the Meridian Grand's south wing.

If a fleet correspondent is owed one moral, it's this: every self-inflicted fire eventually gets its own museum wing. The trick isn't lighting the fuse — any private military corp can manage that on a Tuesday. The trick is outliving the people who could tell you otherwise, long enough to be the one holding the matches at the ribbon-cutting.
