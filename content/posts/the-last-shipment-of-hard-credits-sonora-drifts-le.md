Sonora Drift's Colony Administration pushed the Ledger Integration Act through two Assembly commissions in nine days flat. For a cargo manifest that's fast. For legislation touching every vendor stall on the station, it's faster than the lift cars run on a holiday shift.

The bill instructs the Treasury Office to designate "strategic sectors" where only neural-wallet transfers will be accepted. Fuel depots and orbital lift tolls go first. Nobody will say what goes second, or what happens to a dockhand who still hands a grease-stained chit across the counter after the deadline passes.

The numbers are worth sitting with. By the Administration's own survey, 85 percent of transactions under twenty SGC on Sonora Drift still move in physical chits. That's not nostalgia. That's a population that has learned, over several administrations, that a traceable ledger entry is a taxable ledger entry, and a taxable ledger entry is a line item somebody downstream gets to argue about.

Administrator Serafina Voss calls it convenience: "Citizens won't need to carry chits — they'll pay from the wrist unit, directly, from anywhere." She did not say who clears that transfer, or what toll gets skimmed off the top before it lands.

The answer showed up at the Interstellar Banking Convention three cycles back, where Voss shared a stage with executives from Orion Trust, Pallas Holdings, Helios Mercantile, the Interstellar Settlements Bureau, and a retired Earth Unified Council premier now drawing consulting fees from Obsidian Capital. Orion Trust's regional chair told the trade press his institution was "preparing protocols to accelerate wrist-transfer adoption," and that regulators were "studying measures" to eliminate chits outright at depots and tollgates. He attributed chit preference to colonists "not trusting the Administration's income assessments." He did not propose fixing the trust problem. He proposed removing the option of not trusting it.

Sonora Drift isn't the first frontier settlement to try this manifest. Tanager Drift ran the same play four years back, pulling its high-denomination chits from circulation overnight to force the transition. The resulting freight bottleneck at its market docks lasted eleven weeks, because half the station's small freighters settled accounts in exactly those chits. No one at the Convention mentioned Tanager Drift by name.

The Ledger Integration Act, as drafted, doesn't say what happens to a merchant who keeps accepting chits after the designated-sector rules take effect. It doesn't say who absorbs the processing fee on every forced digital transfer, though freight math suggests it won't be the clearing houses. And it doesn't explain why a station with no power-grid redundancy needs its toll plazas running exclusively on a network that goes dark in the same outages that have hit Sonora Drift's grid four times this cycle.

A ledger that can't clear during a blackout isn't infrastructure. It's a toll booth with one gate and a breaker switch somebody else controls.

When the designated sectors get named in fifteen business days, as the bill requires, Sonora Drift's dockhands will find out whether their grease-stained chits are still legal tender for the cargo they've already loaded. Whose manifest is the Treasury Office actually clearing?
