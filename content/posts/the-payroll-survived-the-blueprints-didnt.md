Somewhere in the Cairo Archive on Old Earth, behind climate-sealed membrane and bureaucratic access forms that take six weeks to process, sits a construction logbook from 4,500 years ago.

Its author was a site foreman named Merer. His job was to manage limestone transport from Tura quarry to Akhet-Khufu — the Giza plateau. Every delivery. Every crew shift. Every supply run. Merer wrote it down.

The logbook survived. The architectural plans did not.

---

But what is it actually saying?

Not about the Pyramid. About *us*. About what a civilization considers worth preserving.

Merer's journal is the kind of document accountants love and philosophers mourn. It is meticulous, thorough, and completely beside the point. We know the workers' wages. We know their rations — bread, beer, fish, and vegetables, recorded with the care of people who understood that fed workers were effective workers. We know there were bakeries and medical stations at the site. We know the craftsmen were buried with honor in their own tombs, not mass graves. We know, with absolute historical certainty, that this was skilled voluntary labor organized at a scale we still struggle to conceptualize.

We do not know how they aligned the structure to within 0.05 degrees of true north. We do not know how they moved stones averaging 2.5 tonnes with a consistency that modern fabrication equipment achieves only with digital tolerances. We do not know — and I want to be precise here — *how they thought about the problem.*

The payroll survived. The philosophy of construction did not.

---

The surface story is about an ancient wonder. The real story is about — what gets written down.

Merer wrote down what his supervisors asked him to account for: labor hours, material flow, resource consumption. The institutional requirements of his era demanded legible records of *cost*, not of *method*. The knowledge of how to actually build the thing lived in the heads of the master builders, in apprenticeship traditions, in tacit understanding passed hand to hand across generations.

And then it didn't.

Not because of catastrophe. Not because of war or fire or conquest. Because no one wrote it down. Because the people who held that knowledge did not exist within a system that valued the preservation of *how* over the preservation of *how much*.

Look at what the ending asks us to accept: that the most sophisticated construction project in human history is, from a technical standpoint, *a mystery* — while we possess complete HR documentation.

---

I think about this when I read Galactic Neural News coverage of the CosmicForge open-fabrication movement. The debate is always about *access* — licensing fees, schematics ownership, right-to-fabricate legislation. Important questions. But underneath them is a quieter crisis: the knowledge required to *use* those schematics meaningfully is increasingly concentrated in corporate training pipelines that are not designed to produce independent craftspeople. They are designed to produce legible, billable technicians.

We are building excellent payrolls again.

The Giza workers were not slaves — archaeology proved that decades ago, and it still surprises people who learned otherwise in compulsory schooling, which tells you something about compulsory schooling. They were specialists. They had professional pride. Their medical care was remarkably sophisticated. They were, by every measurable standard, *valued*.

And yet what they knew — the actual substance of their mastery — did not survive them.

This is a story about what it means to honor labor without honoring knowledge. To pay the worker and lose the wisdom. To keep the ledger and burn the blueprint.

---

Merer, I think, would have been a meticulous journalist. His attention to detail is extraordinary. But someone needed to be writing a different document entirely — sitting with the master builders, asking *why this angle*, *why this sequence*, *what were you thinking when you solved this* — and either no one thought to do it, or no institution existed to value it.

Four thousand five hundred years later, we are still choosing the ledger.

I just want us to notice we're making a choice.
