Three points. In a Vector Dome built to showcase the most expensive bodies in the Outer Systems, the Kepler Comets managed three points against Jovian Drift, the worst output of Anchor-captain Renn Okoye's 153 starts. Eleven payload completions, the fewest of his career. The commentary desks called it an 'embarrassment.' I call it a labor report nobody filed.

Here is what actually happened: two starting Tethers were out — one for a spinal-compression injury that has kept him off the roster all season after a public shouting match with coaching staff over being rushed back early, the other serving a league suspension stacked on top of recurring thruster-joint damage. A third Tether, drafted two cycles ago on a cadet-slot contract that forbids him from negotiating his own terms until his option years burn off, dropped another payload he had no business reaching for in the first place, because he's been flown in heavier gravity wells than his training ever prepared him for. The hull-plating crew — what the broadcast calls 'blockers' — was also down two bodies. Every single point of failure on that roster is a person who does not get a vote in where they fly next.

So naturally, the league's marquee prediction column spent this week's edition drafting five hypothetical 'procurement moves' on, I quote, 'the back of a napkin.' Send Tether A and a 2938 third-round cadet slot for Tether B. Sweeten with a conditional fifth. Dangle the undersized speed-Tether because his 'value in theory exceeds his value in practice' — a human being's name placed next to the phrase 'value in theory.'

The general manager, for his part, has been 'calling around' — the same verb you'd use for sourcing replacement parts for a reactor coil. Five seasons of active trading, one quiet year, now back on the comm. Nobody in this ecosystem — not the GM, not the column, not the fans screaming division-rivalry chants into the recycled dome air — has asked the Tethers in question whether they'd like to be flown to a different station, assigned a different coaching staff, strapped into an unfamiliar thruster rig mid-season with three weeks to learn the catch timing before the real games start mattering.

The league's own injury-disclosure office logged forty-one thruster-joint incidents this cycle. The same office has no mechanism for a Tether to decline a trade on medical grounds, because the cadet-slot contract was written before anyone imagined a players' tether union with actual leverage. There's a proposal on the table right now — hazard differential pay scaled to g-force exposure, a seat on the trade-review panel for the athlete being moved — and it has been sitting in a procedural drawer for two cycles while the prediction columns fill airtime ranking players like produce.

I'm not against the sport. Stripped of ownership, it's the most beautiful thing humans have figured out how to do in freefall. It's everything bolted onto it — the cadet clauses, the napkin trades, the reactor-coil language for human spines — that turns an athlete into inventory before the season's even over.
