I counted the mirrors before I counted the words.

Thirty-one channels, by my last tally, all carrying the same transmission from a man who calls himself a teacher of Natural Law, broadcasting from somewhere in the Kessler Drift. Neural-feed relays, salvaged holoscreen networks, a dozen encrypted system-net nodes that exist, as far as I can tell, so that if the Colony Administration pulls one plug, thirty remain lit. He calls this redundancy "the One Great Work." He calls his audience to "utilize the communication and publishing aspects of computing technology" so that "Objective Morality" becomes "common sense knowledge for everyone." There is a donation tier that gets you a signed data-crystal. There is a gift shop.

I did not go to the Drift. I went instead to a relay shack bolted to the underside of a cargo gantry on Penrose Approach, because I'd heard the woman who runs it does something stranger than broadcasting. She listens.

Mireille Okonkwo-Vass has operated the Penrose relay for forty-one years, bouncing signal between three generations of her own family scattered across eleven systems. Her equipment is older than her youngest grandchild — a bank of magnetic tape decks she refuses to replace because, she says, "tape doesn't lie about what it heard." Every week she patches a channel through to whichever grandchild has a free hour, and every week, without a script, without a channel name, she tells them something true she noticed that week. That the dockhands split tips evenly even though no rule says to. That her neighbor fixed a stranger's air scrubber and wouldn't take payment for it. That fairness, in her experience, doesn't need enforcing so much as noticing.

"Nobody taught me to teach it this way," she told me, adjusting a dial that hissed static into something like a voice. "You just say what you saw."

I asked her, carefully, if she'd heard of the Kessler broadcaster. She had — her youngest grandson sends her clips sometimes, bewildered by the vocabulary. She listened to one all the way through once. "He's not wrong about much of it," she admitted. "But he talks like morality is something you catch, like a signal. Like you need his thirty channels to get it. I don't think it travels that way. I think it travels the way everything worth keeping travels out here — hand to hand, meal to meal, because somebody noticed and said so."

There is no mirror of Mireille's broadcast. No backup channel if the relay fails. If it goes dark, the lesson doesn't reach her grandson that week, and she'll tell him in person next time he visits, which he does, because distance between family out here is measured in waiting, not in loss.

The Kessler broadcaster has an audience of, by his own count, four hundred thousand. Mireille has four grandchildren and a dockhand who listens in sometimes because the signal leaks into the public band. I'm not sure which number means more. I'm fairly sure she isn't counting.
