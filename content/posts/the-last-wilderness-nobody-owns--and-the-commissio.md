Nobody runs Anchorhold Station. That's the whole pitch.

It sits on a moon called Kenaiyu, claimed on paper by both a Core Systems Colony Administration and an Outer Rim trade bloc, and for forty years neither side has finished the paperwork to actually govern it. The result is the strangest thing I've found in three years of chasing stories nobody wants filed: a settlement where you can catch dinner, climb a mountain, and watch megafauna breach without a permit, a chaperone drone, or a Mobi notification reminding you that your recreation window has expired.

**Intake Run.** The river feeds straight into the old fabrication port, and every summer it fills with ghost coho — descendants of Earth salmon embryos some terraforming crew dumped here a century back and never logged. No quota station, no tagging requirement. I watched a woman pull a thirty-pound fish off the bank with a line she'd braided from scavenged tether cable. I bought roe off her at a price that would get laughed out of any Core Systems fish market. Old habits.

**The Coastal Causeway.** Eleven miles, no checkpoints, no toll relay. You can walk it, bike it, or — in the low-grav winters — ski it straight through three biomes without anyone cross-referencing your travel manifest. Moose-analogues use it as a commuting lane. So do I, mostly to avoid whoever's watching the port.

**Silo Park.** This is the one that got me. Fourteen hundred acres of trail wrapped around a decommissioned orbital-defense installation from back when the two administrations actually thought they'd shoot at each other over this moon. The launch tubes are cold storage now — officially for disc-golf equipment, unofficially for cargo that prefers not to clear either jurisdiction's manifest. I know this because a contact walked me through one personally, proud of the climate control.

**The Marsh Annex.** Wetlands downstream of an old outflow line, thick with avian life nobody's bothered to classify, let alone protect. Self-guided, unmonitored, free. Birders come from three systems over and nobody checks their visas.

**Beluga Shelf.** Once a month the moon's thin crust lets a gravitational tide sweep the inlet — six to ten feet of displaced water moving slow enough that riders come from across the Rim to surf it. The void-belugas show up chasing the same fish I was poaching upriver. Nobody's claimed jurisdiction over the whales either.

**The climb.** A shuttle out of the station center — chunky, analog, older than the dispute itself — drops you at the trailhead for the moon's most-climbed peak. From the top you can see both administrations' listening posts, neither of which has ever bothered to post a ranger.

A joint commission convenes next quarter to finally settle ownership. Once it does, expect permits, quotas, safety-compliance fleets, and a toll relay on that causeway. Go now. The gap doesn't stay open forever — I've watched enough of them close to know what's coming.
