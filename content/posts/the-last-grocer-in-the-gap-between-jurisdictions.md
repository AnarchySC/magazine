Nobody on Halkett Point locks their hatch. I noticed this on my second day, when my neighbor Reyna handed me a spare key fob to her habitat "just in case the pressure seal acts up while I'm at the Market." She didn't ask who I was. She'd seen me at the deli counter the day before, which apparently was enough.

The Market she meant is the only one. Officially it's the Point Commissary, 3,100 square meters of pressurized retail that is, without contest, the largest structure in this odd little jurisdictional accident — a Core Systems enclave stranded on the wrong side of an Outer Rim Coalition corridor, connected to the rest of Earth Unified territory by nothing but a thin, contested lane called the Varro Gate.

Lenna Hayes bought the Commissary in year 2901, thirty-four years ago. Before it existed, people here kept chickens, traded with corner stalls, or rode the long way around to Bellhaven Station just to find fresh protein. "We used to be two systems' worth of trouble for a carton of eggs," Hayes told me, ringing up someone's noodle rations without looking up — she already knew the total.

For a long stretch, the arrangement worked. A Core-based wholesaler ships goods that cross into Rim space "in transit," skating through the Varro Gate without duty before re-entering Council jurisdiction a few hundred kilometers from the Commissary's loading dock. On weekends, the parking apron used to fill with more Rim-registered haulers than local ones — Coalition families crossing for cheap reactor fuel and a block of cheese on the way home.

That apron is half-empty now. The twenty-month quarantine during the Drift Fever outbreak hollowed things out first — the corridor stayed open for freight, but nobody wanted to cross a checkpoint during a contagion scare, not even for eggs. "2934 was looking better," Hayes said. "Then the Council started talking about the Point like it was already theirs."

She means the Unified Council's recent habit of describing Rim-adjacent enclaves as "the fifty-first colony in waiting" — a rhetorical flourish back home, apparently, but out here it reads like a threat, and the Coalition has responded by leaning on transit tariffs for anything moving through the Gate. A produce hauler that used to cost pocket change now runs Hayes eleven hundred credits a trip. She's cut deliveries to twice a week and trimmed her hours to match.

What keeps her open isn't a subsidy or a council directive — nobody here has filed for either. It's closer to obligation. "If I close, there's no backup," she said simply, like this settled the matter, which for her it does.

The deli counter, meanwhile, keeps functioning as the only town square Halkett Point has ever had — no mayor, no assembly, just people who happen to be hungry at the same hour comparing notes on seal pressure and corridor delays. This is what it actually looks like when neighbors trust each other. Nobody organized it. Nobody had to.
