I spent two winters running the weigh station out past Vesta Reach, checking manifest chips against cargo registry before anything cleared for the inner lanes. There was a senior inspector there, Old Horvat, who could spend four hours arguing with a hauler over whether the name on a bill of lading matched the name on a birth chip from forty years back, three alphabets removed. He was never wrong about the discrepancy. He was also never once asked the question that mattered: why did a mismatched vowel on a form give him the authority to impound a man's ice?

I think about Horvat every time one of these paperwork wars breaks out among the people who'd like to keep running things.

Delegate Sana Khoreth sits for Kesem Reach, one of the Driftborn settlement seats, and in four days she's up for reseating. This week, Compliance Directorate agents went through her habitation module with a fine-tooth scanner, working off a residency filing her family submitted thirty years ago, when they came in from a mining colony that doesn't exist anymore. The filing lists her under a transliteration nobody uses. The Directorate's own court brief now calls her by that name instead of the one she's used in every session for a decade.

One side calls this what it looks like: a sitting Council Chair leaning on a loud critic through the machinery of law enforcement, days before the public gets to weigh in. They note the filing was reviewed and cleared years ago. They note the Chair's neural-feed history of swinging at Khoreth, and at Driftborn migrants generally. They say if a seated delegate can be turned over like cargo before a vote, nobody who talks back is safe.

The other side says the law doesn't care who you are. Says the questions about her name, her family's papers, her actual standing, were never properly closed. Says if someone built a career on a citizenship that doesn't check out, she has no business writing rules for anyone else.

Both sides are fighting hard about whether Khoreth belongs in that seat. Neither side is fighting about whether the seat should exist to be belonged in.

That's the part Horvat never got to either, bless him. A scale can tell you the mass of a shipment. It cannot tell you who's entitled to own what's inside the container. A registry can tell you whether a name on a chip matches a name on a form. It cannot tell you who earned the right to govern another living person's choices. Run that paperwork through every court and compliance scanner in the Core Systems and you still haven't answered the only question worth asking: with the right chip or without it, where does anyone get off ruling somebody else?

Nobody's raiding that question. It doesn't need a filing cabinet. It just needs someone willing to ask it out loud before the vote, not after.
