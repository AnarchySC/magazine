Okay, picture this: a converted grain dome on the Tri-Array Collective, somewhere between the old Ioway and Missou settlement lines, lit by a CRT countdown clock someone salvaged from a decommissioned shuttle bay. Forty people at one long table. A chef plating something called 'Course Four: T-Minus Ninety Minutes.' Nobody's being ironic. Everybody's taking notes.

This is Countdown Cuisine, and if you haven't heard of it yet, you will.

The setup: Fleet Logistics Command is detonating a legacy stockpile of azide-core munitions at the Mid-Continent Ordnance Yard — leftover hardware nobody's used in two centuries, sitting there because decommissioning costs more political capital than storage. The particulate path modeling puts roughly 350 agricultural collectives inside the projected fallout zone. Under-Director Reynol Fordam, speaking for Agricultural Production and Conservation, called it a 'worst-case scenario, mitigated by Fleet measures,' and promised existing protocols would keep heavy metals out of the supply chain. Classic reassurance architecture — all nouns, no numbers.

The Tri-Array farmers unions asked for something simpler: independent testing, direct notice, a name attached to accountability if the soil comes back wrong. What they got instead was a press release and a scheduled Wednesday morning.

So the growers did what frontier culture always does when institutions go quiet — they built a ritual around the silence.

Countdown Cuisine started as exactly one dinner, thrown by a collective cook who wanted to use the current harvest before anyone could argue later about whether it was 'clean.' Menu built around the clock: each course timed to the public countdown feed, dishes named for the hours remaining. Diners wear vintage radiation-badge brooches as jewelry — pure theater, the badges aren't even calibrated, but that's the point. The accessory isn't measuring anything. It's a protest against not being allowed to measure anything that matters.

The soundtrack is the real tell. Underground tape producers are looping the actual Fleet countdown announcements — that flat, procedural voice reading off minutes — into slow ambient drones. It's spreading through the same channels that carried thermaline gospel out of the fuel-bailout scene. Same engine, different grievance: official calm, weaponized into art by people who weren't asked.

Now it's a format. Supper clubs across three settlement lines are running their own versions ahead of local demolitions, inspections, and depot closures nobody fully explains. The dishes change. The structure doesn't: a clock, a toast, a meal eaten in full knowledge that someone upstream decided your trust was the cheapest thing to spend.

Food movements that start as grief rituals have a way of becoming permanent fixtures — ask anyone who's eaten at a Reinvention Algorithm pop-up. Countdown Cuisine isn't about the lead. It's about who gets to say 'don't worry about it' and walk away before dinner's served.
