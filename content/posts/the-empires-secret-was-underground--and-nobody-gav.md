The schoolbook version goes like this: the Second Dominion of Ferrevan, in its golden age, engineered a marvel — hundreds of kilometers of underground channels carrying glacier melt from the highland ice caps down to the garden terraces of Dastur Flats, powered by nothing but slope and patience. The Dominion's archives, still projected on a looping reel in the settlement's one public terminal, credit "Imperial Hydro-Engineers, Third Census."

Nobody in Dastur Flats believes that reel. They just don't argue with it, because arguing with a reel has never filled a cistern.

I spent three weeks walking the vertical shafts with a channel-keeper named Oyuun Teshvar, whose family has tended the same eleven-kilometer stretch of tunnel for nine generations, by her count, though she admits the count is soft. "Nobody wrote it down," she said, lowering a brass flow-gauge on a hand-cranked spool into a shaft barely wider than her shoulders. "You don't write down a thing everybody already knows."

The channels — locals call them zel-lines, not qanats, though the principle is identical to the old desert word — were dug by hand, shaft by shaft, each one a breathing hole and an access point, so that gravity alone does the carrying. No pumps. No reactor draw. No ministry survey stamp. When a shaft collapses, whoever notices first starts digging, and whoever has a free afternoon joins them. There is a rotation, but no roster. There is a map, but it lives in three elders' heads and gets redrawn from memory whenever someone new is trusted with it.

What struck me wasn't the engineering — though lowering yourself forty meters into cool dark air while a dust storm rages topside will rearrange your opinion of architecture — it was how little anyone needed to be told to maintain it. Oyuun's nephew, nineteen, missed two meals helping reroute a blocked vein last month. Nobody assigned him. He just happened to be standing there when the water stopped.

A licensing agent from Stellar Hydro-Concepts came through last season, mapping the zel-lines for what the company's paperwork called "Heritage Flow Architecture, pending patent." The agent offered the settlement a maintenance contract — for a fee — to keep doing exactly what it had already been doing for nine uncounted generations. Three elders listened politely, nodded, and went back to recalibrating a sediment trap with a bent spoon. The agent left with a sample core and no signature.

The Dominion that supposedly built this is nine centuries gone. Its reel still plays. The water still runs, on a schedule no council set, maintained by people who've never filed a single form for the privilege of being thirsty, and then not being thirsty, together.
