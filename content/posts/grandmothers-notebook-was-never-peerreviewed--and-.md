The ledger lives in a biscuit tin that has outlived two ships and one marriage. Pages of it are tape-spliced where the paper tore. Somewhere around entry four hundred, the handwriting changes from a grandmother's careful cursive to her grandson's hurried shorthand, because he was the one home when the fever broke and she wasn't going to let that go unrecorded.

"We don't publish anything," says Amara Dzevin-Oyelaran, seventy-one standard years, matriarch of a family now scattered across Harrow's Reach, Tessellon Drift, and two orbital clinics near Kepler Freight. "We just write down what happened to us. That's all it ever was supposed to be."

What happened to them, over sixty years, fills eleven notebooks and one battered cassette archive — home remedies, inoculation reactions, which batch of which Concordance-approved compound made Uncle Femi's joints worse instead of better, which neighbor's broth actually brought a child's temperature down when the clinic's gene-script patch did nothing at all. None of it is peer-reviewed. None of it carries a Galactic Medicines Bureau stamp. The family trusts it more than anything printed in the Helix Record, the flagship journal of the loose, well-funded alliance of labs, publishers, and regulators everyone on the Reach simply calls "the Concordance" — the body that decides, for the rest of the galaxy, what counts as proven.

I didn't come looking for a controversy. I came because a conduit technician mentioned, almost offhand, that his family "does its own science," and thought nothing of it. Three weeks in, I understood why. Nobody in this family set out to distrust institutions. They just noticed, trip after migration trip, that what worked for them rarely matched what the glossy bulletins said should work — and that raising the discrepancy out loud, in public channels, tended to end careers faster than it ended symptoms.

"My cousin asked one honest question at a settlement health forum," says Teodor, the grandson with the hurried shorthand. "Within a week he was 'the heretic from Tessellon.' Nobody debated him. They just stopped inviting him." So the family stopped asking the Concordance anything. They started asking each other.

What strikes me, watching them pass the tin around a kitchen table under three different suns' worth of photographs, is how unremarkable they find this. No manifesto, no movement — just the quiet, almost embarrassed admission that a notebook kept by people who love you is sometimes more honest than a pipeline built to keep paying customers satisfied. Dr. Oyelaran-Finch, the family's one credentialed physician, still consults the official literature — she has to, for her license — but she keeps the tin on her desk too. "The journals tell me what's approved," she says. "The tin tells me what's true for the people I actually know."

Nobody organized this family into a system of trust. They built one anyway, one shaky entry at a time, because waiting for an institution with a stake in the answer stopped making sense somewhere around the second lightyear.
