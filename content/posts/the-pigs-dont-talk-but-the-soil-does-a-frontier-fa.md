The Voss paddock shifts every eleven hours. Not because a regulator said so — there is no regulator on Kessandra Drift, not for this — but because Mireille Voss watched her father watch his father, and all three of them watched the ground.

"You leave them too long, you get a moonscape," she tells me, nodding at a strip of silvopasture that two days ago was dense understory and today is churned, aerated, already greening back from the roots up. "You move them too soon, nothing gets disturbed enough to matter. There's a window. Maybe four hours wide. Nobody can give you that window on a chart. You have to feel it."

The hogs themselves are not what the catalogue breed-registries back on the Core Systems would recognize. Torpedo-shaped, low to the ground, deep through the chest — bred over four generations of station drift not for a certificate but for what Mireille calls "performance in the dirt." A cousin line down in the valley kept the papers, kept the registered bloodline, and lost forty animals to foot rot in a single wet season. "Paper doesn't root out a parasite cycle," her brother Teo says. "Phenotype does. You keep what survives your actual land, not what wins a ribbon somewhere else."

What strikes me, sitting at their kitchen table with a mug of something fermented and faintly medicinal, is how little of this knowledge came from any institution at all. No Colony Administration extension office sent an inspector. No licensing board approved the rotation schedule stapled to the barn wall, hand-drawn, half-smudged. The Voss family — and, it turns out, nineteen other households up and down the drift, trading notes over a battered mesh-net relay that drops signal every time the weather turns — built this entire system the way you'd expect ordinary people to build anything they actually depend on: by comparing notes, admitting failures out loud, and copying whatever worked for the neighbor down the ridge.

"We talk more to each other than anybody talks to a protein vat operator," Teo says, meaning the big enclosed feeding rigs the mega-corps run near the station core, where animals never touch open ground and the only variable anyone tracks is throughput per credit. The Voss operation runs on a different arithmetic entirely — land-extensive, slow, deliberately unoptimized in the sense the vat operators would mean it. They don't chase the commodity board's weekly protein index. They sell directly, station to station, on relationships that took a decade to build and could evaporate in a season of dishonesty.

Mireille walks me to the fence line at dusk. The hogs are rooting contentedly in ground that, by tomorrow, they'll have moved off of entirely, leaving it to rest for forty days before anything touches it again. Nobody timed this interval in a lab. Nobody needed to. "The land tells you when it's ready," she says. "You just have to be the kind of person who's still listening."
