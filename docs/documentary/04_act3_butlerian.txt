Here's the uncomfortable part. The cassette future didn't die because it couldn't work. It was outvoted. We chose the other fork. We chased the shrinking chip, and the sealed case, and the screen that does everything by pretending to be nothing. We traded the switch for the swipe, the part for the subscription, the thing you owned for the thing you rent. But a fork is not a wall. And one of our best storytellers imagined the road we didn't take. In Frank Herbert's Dune, humanity's history turns on an event called the Butlerian Jihad — a revolt against the thinking machines, ending in a single, civilization-defining commandment. Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind. It did not end progress. It redirected it. Denied the crutch of artificial minds, that civilization poured its genius into human faculties, and honest tools — trained minds instead of black boxes, instruments you could read, mastery instead of automation. Now run that thought forward, here. Imagine a directive — a simple, deliberate brake on thinking machines. Not a rejection of technology. A rule about what kind. The engineers don't go home. They get bored — and then they get brilliant, in the only directions left to them. Analog storage, perfected instead of abandoned. Mechanical computation, refined for a century. Interfaces built for human hands, because there's no algorithm to build them for. Tools designed to be understood, owned, and repaired — because a machine you can't understand is exactly the machine the directive forbids. That's the revelation cassette futurism has been hiding all along. It was never nostalgia. It's a blueprint — for a future we're still allowed to choose. And the first tremors are already here. In the right-to-repair fights. In the analog revival. In a growing unease about machines built in the likeness of our own minds. Small refusals. The beginning, maybe, of a directive we write for ourselves.
